## Layman Summary

This experiment analyzes whether changes in word frequency patterns (Zipfian curvature) correspond to shifts in meaning across sections of Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, finding no significant linear relationship.

## Full Summary

This experiment implements a computational linguistics methodology to test the hypothesis that Zipf-Mandelbrot curvature parameter drift correlates with semantic drift in narrative text. Using Pride and Prejudice as a case study, the analysis splits the novel into 10 sections and for each: (1) tokenizes into rolling windows of 500 tokens; (2) fits Zipf-Mandelbrot distributions via Maximum Likelihood Estimation, extracting exponent (s) and curvature (b) parameters; (3) computes semantic drift as cosine distance between consecutive window centroids using deterministic 300-dim embeddings; (4) calculates curvature drift as first differences of the b parameter; (5) tests Pearson and Spearman correlations between curvature drift and semantic drift; (6) establishes significance against 10 shuffled-text baselines per section. Results across 10 sections show weak, non-significant correlations for Zipf-Mandelbrot MLE (mean Pearson r = -0.025 ± 0.075) that are not significantly different from shuffled baselines (mean significance p = 0.48 ± 0.23). The standard Zipf power-law baseline is degenerate (b=0 fixed, NaN correlations for all sections), confirming the necessity of the 2-parameter Zipf-Mandelbrot model. Total of 20 examples (10 sections × 2 methods) with full per-section results.
